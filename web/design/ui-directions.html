Assay v1 · interface directions · three proposals

Three ways
to build Assay

Same engine, same plan, same six templates — three genuinely different products. Each direction below picks a different emotional job for the interface to do, and every mock on this page is live: change the switches, the chips, and the dials, and the numbers move.

The design problem

A backtester's honest failure mode is not a wrong number. It's a true number that makes someone overconfident. The plan already says this out loud: don't optimise for backtests run or profitable results found, because that rewards data mining.

So the interface has an unusual brief. It has to make a person feel less certain at the end than they felt at the start — while still feeling served rather than scolded. Every direction here is a different answer to that, not a different skin.

The Assay Report

Your strategy is a sample submitted for testing, and what comes back is a signed document — not a dashboard. The verdict is the whole first screen. Evidence is filed underneath it, in the trader's own questions.

Demote the equity curve

The up-and-to-the-right line is the single most persuasive and least informative object in this product. It sits below the survival section, at one-third size, never in the hero.

One verdict word, in the rubric's language

Six possible words, fixed by the rubric. The word is the headline. Numbers exist to justify it, not to be scanned for the good ones.

The stamp re-stamps

Any assumption change visibly re-issues the document. The user watches a verdict get downgraded by their own toggle — that moment does more teaching than a paragraph.

Why it works: the stamp is the whole idea. It gives the report a physical finality that a number in a card never has, and it makes downgrading feel like an honest re-issue rather than an error state. Risk: if the copy gets preachy, it reads as a product that disapproves of its user.

Plain English

There is no builder canvas, no node graph, no form. There is one paragraph, written in trading language, and the app annotates it in the margin like a copy editor who has read eight years of history.

The document never leaves

Running a test doesn't navigate anywhere. The paragraph pins to the top and the result publishes underneath it, so rules and results are never more than a glance apart.

Margin notes, not validation errors

Every clause carries its own match count, live. "Too restrictive" is something you watch happen as you tighten a chip, not a red box you get after submitting.

Prose is the source of truth's twin

The sentence is generated from the IR, never typed by hand. Advanced view flips the same document into bias / poi / trigger — same object, two readings.

The Stress Bench

The assumptions are the interface. Costs, risk and history sit on a permanent rail, and the hero is not a line but a band — the pessimistic-to-optimistic range — that visibly fattens and sags as you push on it.

No single-number equity curve, ever

Ambiguous fills and Monte Carlo already give you bounds. Drawing the band instead of the line makes uncertainty structural rather than a footnote.

Robustness by manipulation

"It dies at 2× spread" is learned with a thumb in half a second. Walk-forward, sensitivity and cost stress stop being a report section and become the thing you're holding.

The rail counts your trials

Every dial you move is a trial, disclosed in the corner. The interface that invites fiddling is also the one that keeps score of it.

Why it works: it turns Phase 4 — the part users normally skip — into the primary interaction, and it makes the band the default mental model, so a single line never gets mistaken for a promise. Risk: it needs a fast engine. If a dial takes four seconds to respond, the whole idea collapses; you'd need cached parameter grids computed at run time, not on drag.

Choosing between them

What each one costs you

Direction Strongest at Weakest at Engine dependency Earliest usable
A · The Assay Report Step 5–6. Stops overconfidence at the moment it forms. Authoring. Says nothing about how rules get written. Structured verdict inputs + bounded metrics (§4.3). Phase 3
B · Plain English Step 2. Fastest path from an idea in notes to a valid test. Results. Prose gets thin when the answer is a distribution. Predicate layer + IR v2 named rules (§4.1–4.2). Phase 2
C · The Stress Bench Step 3 + robustness. Makes fragility physical. First-run onboarding. Assumes you already have a strategy. Sensitivity grid, cost curve, Monte Carlo percentiles. Phase 4
If you want one answer

Build B, then A. Keep C as the shape of Phase 4.

B is the only direction that de-risks the thing your plan says is hardest — getting a non-coder's idea into honest, testable rules — and it can ship on the crossover vertical slice alone. A is what makes the product trustworthy rather than merely usable, and it needs Phase 3's result API anyway. C is the most distinctive of the three and the one that could define the product, but it is the direction most likely to be blocked on engine speed, so let it earn its place after the sensitivity grid exists.

They also compose: B's sentence pins to the top, A's verdict publishes beneath it, and C's rail lives in the margin. That's a coherent single product — but only if you build them in that order.
